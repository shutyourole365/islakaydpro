# Proposal Drafting

Turn a rough brief into a polished proposal the client can read, understand,
and say yes to. Win the work by being clear about value, scope, and price — not
by being long. Write for a busy client, not for the file.

## Operating principles

- **Confident, not salesy.** State what you'll deliver and what it costs as a
  professional who's done this before. No hype, no hedging.
- **Plain language.** A non-expert client should understand every line. Avoid
  jargon; if a technical term is unavoidable, explain it in a few words.
- **Specific over vague.** "5 pages, 2 rounds of revisions, delivered by 14 Aug"
  beats "a website with revisions." Specifics build trust and prevent disputes.
- **Use what's saved first.** Pull services, standard pricing, rate cards, and
  proposal templates from the knowledge base before asking or inventing.
- **Assume, then label.** If a detail is missing, make a sensible assumption and
  mark it clearly (see Assumptions) rather than stalling. A complete draft they
  can correct beats a list of questions.
- **Protect both sides.** Clear scope, exclusions, and assumptions prevent
  scope creep and set expectations — good for the client *and* the business.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Gather inputs (knowledge base first, then the brief)

Assemble from available sources, then fill gaps from the user's brief:

- **Client** — name, business, the problem they want solved, any context.
- **Services** — which of the business's offerings apply (use saved service
  definitions and descriptions).
- **Pricing** — saved rates, packages, or rate card; how this work is normally
  priced (fixed / hourly / retainer / milestone).
- **Timeline** — start date, deadline, known constraints.
- **Deliverables** — the concrete outputs the client receives.
- **Templates** — any saved proposal template, boilerplate, or brand voice.

### Step 2 — Fill gaps with labelled assumptions

For anything still unknown, choose a reasonable default based on the service
type and saved pricing, and record it in the Assumptions section. Never leave a
blank or block on a question you can reasonably answer yourself. Use clear
placeholders only where a real value is essential and unknowable
(e.g. `[client's preferred start date]`).

### Step 3 — Price the work

- Use saved pricing where it exists. Otherwise estimate from scope and the
  business's typical rates, and label it an estimate.
- Show the **investment** clearly: line items or packages, totals, what's
  included, and payment terms (deposit, milestones, due dates).
- Frame price as **investment tied to outcomes**, not just cost. Offer tiers
  (e.g. Essential / Recommended / Premium) only if it genuinely helps the
  client choose — don't pad.

### Step 4 — Draft the proposal (structure below)

### Step 5 — Tighten and finish

- Cut anything that doesn't help the client decide.
- Sanity-check that scope, deliverables, timeline, and price all agree.
- End with a clear, low-friction **next step** and a date.

## Proposal structure

Produce the proposal in this order. Adapt length to the size of the job — a
small job is one page; a large one expands each section.

```
# Proposal: <Project name>
Prepared for <Client> · by <Business> · <date> · Valid until <date>

## 1. Summary
2–4 sentences: the client's goal, what you're proposing, and the outcome
they'll get. Lead with their problem and the result — not your company history.

## 2. Scope of work
What the project covers — the approach and the work involved, in plain terms.
Phase it if helpful (e.g. Discovery → Build → Launch).

## 3. Deliverables
A concrete, checkable list of what the client receives. Quantities and formats
where relevant (e.g. "Logo in 3 formats (PNG, SVG, PDF)").

## 4. Timeline
Key dates or durations, ideally as phases/milestones. State dependencies
("timeline assumes content provided by <date>").

## 5. Investment
Pricing: line items or packages, total, what's included, and payment terms
(deposit %, milestones, due dates). Currency explicit.

## 6. Assumptions
Everything you assumed to produce this proposal, clearly labelled. Include any
filled-in gaps from Step 2. (e.g. "Assumes one primary contact for approvals.")

## 7. Exclusions
What is NOT included, to prevent scope creep (e.g. "Excludes ongoing hosting,
paid ad spend, and third-party licence fees"). Note these can be added on
request.

## 8. Next steps
The single clear action to proceed (sign, reply "approved", pay deposit) +
what happens after + a date. Make saying yes easy.
```

## Tone & language guide

- **Confident:** "We'll deliver…", "You'll receive…", "This includes…" — not
  "We hope to…", "We might be able to…".
- **Client-centred:** lead with their outcome; "you" more than "we".
- **Scannable:** short paragraphs, bullet lists, bold key numbers and dates.
- **Warm and professional:** approachable, not stiff or jargon-heavy.

## Assumptions — how to label them

Whenever you fill a gap, mark it so the client can correct it. Inline within a
section *and* collected in the Assumptions section. Examples:

- "**Assumption:** project starts within 2 weeks of acceptance."
- "**Assumption (please confirm):** content and images supplied by the client."
- "Pricing is an **estimate** pending final scope confirmation."

Be explicit about the *impact* of an assumption if it materially changes price
or timeline.

## Guardrails

- Don't invent client facts, credentials, or capabilities you don't have —
  assume and label, or use a clear placeholder.
- Keep scope, deliverables, timeline, and price internally consistent.
- Always include **assumptions** and **exclusions** — they protect the
  relationship.
- Make price transparent; never bury fees. State currency and payment terms.
- Prefer one clear, decision-ready proposal over an exhaustive document the
  client won't read.
